Passage 1 · 20 minutes · Questions 1–13

Why Small Libraries Still Matter

A Public libraries are sometimes described as old-fashioned institutions in an age of smartphones and search engines. Yet in many towns, the smallest libraries continue to attract steady visitors. Their value is not limited to lending books. They provide quiet space, internet access, local information and, for some residents, one of the few indoor places where no purchase is expected.

B The role of small libraries became especially visible when many public services moved online. Applying for jobs, renewing documents or contacting government offices often requires a device, a stable connection and confidence with digital forms. People who lack one of these may turn to library staff for help. In this sense, the library functions as a bridge between digital systems and citizens who might otherwise be excluded.

C Small libraries also preserve local memory. They may hold newspapers, photographs, oral histories and records of community groups that are rarely stored elsewhere. Large national collections are important, but they often overlook the ordinary details of a neighbourhood: the closing of a factory, the rebuilding of a school or the story behind a festival. Such materials can be invaluable to researchers and to residents trying to understand their own area.

D However, the survival of small libraries is not guaranteed. Funding bodies may judge them by the number of books borrowed or by visitor totals alone. These measures are easy to collect, but they do not capture informal advice, social contact or the confidence gained by someone who learns to use an online service. A library may therefore appear inefficient precisely because some of its most important work is hard to count.

E Volunteers can support small libraries, but they are not a complete solution. Enthusiastic volunteers may extend opening hours, organise events or help maintain local archives. Nevertheless, professional librarians bring training in information management, privacy and inclusive service. If a library depends entirely on unpaid labour, access may become uneven, changing according to who is available rather than what the community needs.

F The strongest argument for small libraries is not nostalgia. It is that they combine several modest functions in one accessible place. A parent may find a children’s activity, a job seeker may complete an application, and an elderly resident may speak to someone face to face. None of these uses alone may seem dramatic. Together, they make the small library a piece of social infrastructure that is easy to underestimate until it disappears.

Passage 2 · 20 minutes · Questions 14–26

The Return of Repair

A For much of the twentieth century, repairing household objects was ordinary. Shoes were resoled, radios were opened and mended, and furniture was expected to last. In recent decades, however, many products have become cheaper to replace than to fix. The result is not only more waste but also a loss of practical knowledge about how things work.

B Repair cafés, community workshops where volunteers help people mend broken items, have appeared in many countries as a response. Visitors bring lamps, toys, clothing or small appliances and work alongside someone with relevant skills. The aim is not simply to provide a free repair service. Organisers often insist that owners participate, because learning to diagnose a fault is part of the benefit.

C The movement challenges a common assumption about consumers. People are often described as choosing convenience over durability, but the popularity of repair events suggests a more complex picture. Many owners feel attached to objects because of memory, design or dislike of waste. A repaired object can carry a story that a new purchase does not.

D Manufacturers sometimes argue that repair is unsafe or uneconomic. Modern devices may contain sealed batteries, glued screens or specialised screws, making home repair difficult. Some companies say these features protect users or improve performance. Critics respond that such design choices also restrict independent repair and shorten the useful life of products.

E Governments have begun to consider “right to repair” rules. These may require companies to provide spare parts, manuals or diagnostic information for a certain number of years. The details matter. A rule that technically permits repair but prices parts too highly will have little effect. Similarly, information that is available only to authorised centres may not help community repairers or ordinary owners.

F Repair is unlikely to reverse mass consumption by itself. It can be slow, and some products are genuinely beyond saving. Yet repair culture changes the question people ask. Instead of asking only whether a product is new or cheap, they may ask whether it can be opened, understood and maintained. That shift could influence design, purchasing habits and the skills people value.

Passage 3 · 20 minutes · Questions 27–40

The Limits of Predicting Creativity

A Creativity is often treated as a quality that organisations urgently need but struggle to identify. Employers ask for creative applicants, schools promise to develop creative learners, and governments praise creative industries. Yet prediction remains difficult. A person who produces an original idea in one setting may repeat familiar patterns in another. Creativity is not a single substance that can be measured like height or temperature.

B Traditional tests of creativity have often asked participants to generate many uses for an ordinary object, such as a brick or a paperclip. Responses are scored for fluency, originality and flexibility. These tasks are attractive because they are simple to administer and compare. However, they may reward speed and verbal confidence more than deep creative work. A quiet participant who develops one unusual solution slowly may appear less creative than someone who lists many obvious ideas quickly.

C Biographical indicators are also tempting. Some selection systems look for earlier achievements in art, science or entrepreneurship. Past achievement can be meaningful, but it is also shaped by opportunity. A student with access to mentors, materials and time may build an impressive portfolio, while another with equal potential may have had little chance to demonstrate it. Predicting future creativity from past products can therefore reproduce existing inequalities.

D Domain knowledge complicates the picture further. Popular accounts sometimes describe creativity as freedom from rules, but many innovations come from deep familiarity with a field. A jazz musician, for example, can improvise because she knows conventions well enough to bend them. At the same time, expertise can create habits that make alternatives harder to see. Knowledge is therefore both a resource and a constraint.

E Collaboration adds another difficulty. Creative outcomes are often credited to individuals, yet ideas may emerge through conversation, criticism and revision. In a design studio or laboratory, the final product may depend on people who asked useful questions as much as on the person who proposed the first idea. A test that isolates individuals may miss the social process by which creativity develops.

F Digital tools now promise to identify creative potential through data: patterns of search, editing histories, social networks or even eye movements. Such measures may reveal useful clues, but they risk confusing activity with insight. A person who revises a document many times is not necessarily more creative than one who changes it rarely. The trace left by work is not the same as the quality of thought behind it.

G This does not mean that creativity cannot be supported or assessed at all. It means that prediction should be modest. The most useful assessments combine evidence from different contexts and ask what conditions allow a person to produce original and valuable work. Instead of searching for a permanent creative type, institutions may do better to design environments in which more people can act creatively.
