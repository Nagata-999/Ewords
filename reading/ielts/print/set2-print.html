Passage 1 · 20 minutes · Questions 1–13

Bringing Urban Streams Back to the Surface

A For much of the twentieth century, city engineers treated small streams as obstacles. Waterways were straightened, confined in concrete channels or buried inside pipes so that roads and buildings could be constructed above them. The practice reduced local flooding in some places, but it also separated residents from water and removed habitats used by insects, fish and birds. In recent years, several cities have begun reversing this process through a practice commonly known as daylighting.

B Daylighting does not simply mean opening a pipe and allowing water to run freely. Engineers must first study how rainfall moves through the entire catchment. A buried stream may now receive far more water than it did before urban development because roofs and paved surfaces prevent rain from soaking into the ground. If a new channel is too narrow, nearby properties may flood. Designers therefore create broad banks, shallow wetlands and temporary storage areas that can hold water after storms.

C Supporters often emphasise ecological gains. Sunlight allows aquatic plants to grow, while varied depths and slower sections of water create shelter for different species. Yet recovery is rarely immediate. Soil around former industrial land may be polluted, and fish cannot return if barriers remain farther downstream. Successful projects connect several pieces of habitat rather than improving one isolated section.

D The social effects can be equally important. A restored stream may become the centre of a walking route or small park, giving residents a cooler place during hot weather. In one neighbourhood, shop owners initially feared that construction would reduce parking and discourage customers. After the stream opened, however, longer visits by pedestrians increased trade for cafés and small stores. This result is encouraging, but it cannot be assumed in every location.

E Costs are a major obstacle. Moving roads, protecting underground cables and purchasing private land can make daylighting more expensive than repairing an old pipe. On the other hand, conventional drainage systems also require maintenance and may fail during unusually intense rain. Some authorities now compare projects over several decades rather than choosing whichever option has the lowest initial price.

F Not every daylighting scheme produces a continuous blue stream. In dry climates, water may appear only after rain, and designers sometimes favour a chain of planted basins rather than a permanently flowing channel. This can disappoint residents who imagined a river throughout the year, but an intermittent system may be more faithful to local hydrology. Explaining this early helps prevent an attractive illustration from becoming an unrealistic promise.

G Maintenance changes after construction. A buried pipe can be inspected according to a fixed engineering schedule, whereas an open channel accumulates litter, sediment and fast-growing vegetation. Community clean-up days may strengthen local attachment, but essential flood protection cannot depend solely on volunteers. Authorities need a long-term budget and a clear division of responsibility between drainage teams, park staff and environmental specialists.

H Evaluation should extend beyond counting wildlife or visitors in the first year. Vegetation matures slowly, rainfall varies between years and surrounding development continues to change. Monitoring plans may track water temperature, flood storage, species and public use, but each indicator answers a different question. A project can succeed as a park while delivering limited ecological connection, or reduce flood risk without attracting many visitors. Publishing several measures prevents one visible result from standing in for the whole project.

I The strongest projects involve residents early. Local people may know where water once appeared after storms or which paths children use to reach school. Their knowledge does not replace technical surveys, but it can reveal problems that maps overlook. Daylighting is therefore best understood not as an attempt to recreate a perfectly natural past, but as a negotiation between water, wildlife and the modern city.

The Quiet Growth of Seed Libraries

A A seed library is usually a cupboard or set of drawers from which people can take packets of seed without buying them. Many operate inside public libraries, community centres or gardens. Users are encouraged, though not always required, to save seed from the plants they grow and return some after harvest. The name suggests a simple borrowing system, but living material behaves differently from books: seeds reproduce, cross with nearby plants and gradually change.

B The earliest community seed exchanges were often created to protect local crop varieties that commercial catalogues no longer sold. A bean maintained by several families for generations might be well adapted to a short growing season or a particular soil. Such varieties can carry useful genetic diversity. This does not mean that every local seed performs better than a modern commercial one; rather, keeping many varieties prevents agriculture from depending on a narrow genetic base.

C Running a reliable collection requires care. Some plants, including peas and tomatoes, usually pollinate themselves, so their seed is comparatively easy to save. Others readily cross-pollinate. If several varieties of squash grow close together, the next generation may not resemble the fruit from which its seed was taken. Libraries therefore provide simple instructions about distance, timing and labelling.

D Storage presents another difficulty. Warmth and moisture shorten the life of most seeds. Coordinators may dry returned seed and keep it in sealed containers, but few small projects can test every packet for germination or disease. For this reason, seed libraries generally describe themselves as community experiments rather than professional seed banks. The latter maintain controlled conditions and detailed records for long-term conservation.

E The educational value may exceed the number of packets exchanged. Workshops teach people how flowers are pollinated, why some crops produce seed only in their second year and how food plants have travelled between regions. Novice gardeners also share failures, which reduces the feeling that unsuccessful crops are simply the result of personal incompetence.

F The meaning of a successful return also needs clarification. A packet may contain too few seeds, carry an incorrect label or produce plants with unexpected characteristics. Libraries that punish such mistakes discourage participation, so many ask contributors to describe uncertainty honestly. A note saying that a variety may have crossed with another is more useful than a confident but unreliable claim. In this way, the collection teaches observation as well as preservation.

G Digital records can help coordinators follow which varieties are popular and which disappear after one season. Nevertheless, complicated registration systems may exclude casual gardeners, defeating the open character of the project. Some libraries therefore record only basic details: crop, variety name, year and neighbourhood. The modest database is not a scientific catalogue, but it can reveal patterns and encourage contributors to return seed from underrepresented crops.

H Climate may gradually alter the traits that gardeners value. A variety preserved unchanged in a freezer records past diversity, whereas seed grown each year can continue adapting through selection. The two strategies serve different purposes and should not be confused. Community libraries mainly support active cultivation and exchange; formal seed banks protect carefully documented material against future loss. Cooperation between them can connect everyday use with long-term conservation.

I Legal questions have occasionally arisen because commercial seed is regulated in many countries. Rules intended to ensure that farmers receive accurately labelled, healthy products may not fit informal, free exchanges. In most cases, authorities have distinguished community sharing from commercial sales, although organisers still need to avoid making unsupported claims about purity or performance. The future of seed libraries will depend on maintaining this balance between openness and responsibility.

Passage 3 · 20 minutes · Questions 27–40

Why Night Trains Are Returning

A For several decades, overnight passenger trains appeared to be disappearing from Europe. Cheap flights covered long distances quickly, while high-speed daytime rail connected major cities. Sleeping cars were expensive to maintain and railway companies preferred vehicles that could make several journeys each day. Recently, however, night services have returned to a number of routes, attracting both enthusiastic travellers and cautious transport planners.

B Environmental concern is one reason. On many routes, rail produces lower carbon emissions per passenger than flying, particularly when trains use electricity from low-carbon sources. Yet the comparison is not automatic. Nearly empty trains perform poorly, and the construction and maintenance of rail infrastructure also require energy. The strongest environmental case arises when night trains attract passengers who would otherwise fly.

C Time is another part of their appeal. A night train may take ten or twelve hours, but much of that time is spent sleeping. It can also carry passengers from one city centre to another, avoiding distant airports and early check-in. For travellers who value a full working day at both ends, a slower vehicle can therefore save usable time.

D Operating such services remains complicated. Trains may cross several national networks, each with different signalling rules, access charges and planned engineering work. A delay in one country can cause a train to lose its scheduled path in the next. Operators also need staff trained for overnight duties and depots able to clean beds, replace linen and supply water within a short turnaround.

E Ticketing can confuse customers. Some journeys combine a basic fare with a reservation for a seat, couchette or private compartment. Prices may change according to demand, and international tickets are not always available through every booking system. New operators have tried to simplify the process, but cooperation between rail companies remains essential.

F Rolling stock is another constraint. Many sleeping cars were withdrawn or converted when services declined, and building replacements takes years. Modern passengers expect power sockets, reliable climate control and secure compartments, while operators must also meet updated fire and accessibility standards. Refurbishing older vehicles is quicker, but it can preserve narrow corridors and steps that are difficult for travellers with limited mobility.

G Public subsidy is debated because night trains serve both transport and tourism. Critics argue that scarce rail funding should increase frequent regional services used every day. Supporters respond that aviation and roads also receive public support and that an international train can reduce demand for short flights. The answer depends partly on occupancy: generous support for mostly empty luxury cabins would be difficult to justify, while a well-used mixed train may deliver broader benefits.

H Demand is also highly seasonal. Summer weekends and holiday periods may sell out while midweek winter departures remain quiet. Operators can vary train length, but removing or adding sleeping cars across borders is operationally difficult. Marketing a route to tourists alone may produce sharp peaks, so companies also seek business travellers, students and people visiting family. A diverse passenger base makes year-round operation more stable.

I Night trains are unlikely to replace aviation across very long distances, nor are they suitable for every passenger. Shared compartments may feel unfamiliar, and accessible cabins are limited on older vehicles. Their revival instead shows that transport choices depend on more than speed. Comfort, city-centre access, emissions and the productive use of time can make an apparently old-fashioned service relevant again.
